Design teardown · Retro Bowl vs Retro Buhurt · 10 Sep 2026

What Retro Bowl actually does, and what we should take

A system-by-system directory of Retro Bowl's design, then an honest comparison against Retro Buhurt as it stands at 94 passing checks. The short version: their fan meter is doing far more work than our notoriety, and they have an entire career layer we have not built.

Part one · their economy

The whole game is one chain, and it has exactly one faucet

Retro Bowl has a single currency, a single source of it, and no money system at all. Everything else in their design hangs off this chain. Read it left to right — this is the finding that matters most for your question about fan percentages.

  1. InputWin or loseThe only input. A loss pays nothing at all.
  2. MeterFan support %Rises on a win, falls on a loss, carries across seasons.
  3. BandsThree tiers0–33% → 1 credit. 33–66% → 2. 66–100% → 3.
  4. OutputCoaching CreditsPer game. The only currency in the game.
  5. Spent onEverythingFacilities, players, staff, cap space, morale.

The fan meter is an income throttle wearing an approval rating's clothes

It has exactly one output: how many credits a game pays. It is not a fail state, not a gate on content, not a reputation that unlocks anything. It does not affect play on the field, there is no home advantage, and it does not generate money because there is no money. The feeling is "the city believes in me". The function is a 3× multiplier on the drip.

A dominant season is roughly 70 credits. A poor one is 16–20. That ~3.5× spread is the entire mechanical footprint of the fan system, and it is what makes a bad year expensive without ever ending your run. documented

The cleverest thing in their whole design

The Stadium is the purchasable answer to variance. You spend the currency the fan bar produces in order to make the fan bar less volatile, which produces more currency. It is a positive feedback loop the player pays into voluntarily, and it is why every guide says stadium first.

And it saturates. Once you are parked in the 66–100% band you are capped at 3 credits a game, and further stadium levels do nothing but protect you through a losing run. The first upgrades feel enormous — a 3× income jump — and the last feel like nothing, which quietly rotates the player's attention onto the roster without any explicit gating. That is a real design trick and we do not have anything like it.

Part one · morale

Morale is a separate meter, and the split is deliberate

Sitting directly below the fan bar is a second percentage: team morale. Two meters, two systems, and the difference between them is the sharpest design decision in the game.

FansMorale
NatureExternal, economicInternal, mechanical
OutputCurrency, and nothing elseFumbles, missed tackles, contract refusals
LeverThe StadiumTraining facility, credits, press choices
SpeedSlow, cumulative, asymmetricFast, per-player
Buyable?No. Only winning moves it.Yes. A meeting costs a few credits.

One is buyable and one is not, on purpose

Morale you can fix with credits. Condition you can fix with credits. Cap space you can buy with credits. Fans you can only fix by winning — and the credits you need to build a winning roster come from fans. That is the one genuinely locked door in their economy, and it is what produces the widely reported "season six is when the game actually begins" experience.

If morale were also unbuyable the game would be punishing. If fans were also buyable there would be no economy. The split gives the player a lever to pull every single week while the strategic constraint stays honest.

Morale bands: Toxic below 10%, then Bad, Poor, O.K. at 50%, Good at 70%, Great at 80%, Exceptional at 90%. A toxic player cannot be traded and will not sign an extension until you pay to lift him, and he drags team morale down while he sits there. documented

One upgrade per facility per week

A hard throttle. You cannot dump a windfall into infrastructure — a Retro Bowl win or a pick sale cannot be converted into instant progress. Facility investment is forced to spread across a season regardless of how rich you are. It is a small rule doing a lot of pacing work. community

Part one · the roster

Ten men for twenty-two positions

This is their central squeeze and it is far more brutal than ours.

Roster
10 star players free, 12 with the $0.99 unlock. No per-position minimums — you can roster zero quarterbacks if you want.
On the field
11 on offence, 11 on defence, plus a kicker. Twenty-three slots.
Nonames
Every slot you have not filled is played by an unnamed, unrated player with the worst possible stats. He accrues no stats and cannot be managed.
Positions
Nine: QB, RB, WR, TE, OL, DL, LB, DB, K. No punter — the kicker punts.
Attributes
Exactly four per player, scale 1–10: one position-specific skill, then Strength, Speed, Stamina.
Overall
0.5 to 5.0 stars in half-star steps.

The noname is the best idea in their roster design

It converts "who do I sign" into "which twelve of twenty-three positions do I refuse to leave to a scrub", and it makes every single roster slot a visible, painful trade-off with no accounting required. There is no bench management, no squad depth screen, no rotation — the replacement level is simply terrible and always present.

Potential is a separate number, and only one thing raises it

Every player carries a current star rating and a potential star rating, both on the same 0.5–5.0 scale, plus per-attribute headroom shown as the hollow part of each bar. A 2.5-star rookie with 4.5-star potential is an asset; a 3.5-star veteran with 3.5-star potential is finished.

Players gain XP from production — passing yards, tackles, sacks — and are docked for sacks taken, interceptions and fumbles. A level-up gives +1 to one attribute. Once every attribute is maxed, further level-ups pay out credits instead, which is why the community farms offensive linemen as credit generators.

The only way to raise potential itself is a coordinator with the Talent Spotter trait: +0.5 stars of potential, once per hire, distributed across random attributes. Hiring a new Talent Spotter triggers it again. This single trait is the basis of the entire high-level meta — you draft a player with low overall potential but high potential in the one attribute you care about, then Talent-Spotter him upward. community

Ageing is confirmed by the developer

From the official account: over-30s lose stamina and one of either strength or speed at the end of every season. Mandatory retirement at 40 for quarterbacks and kickers, 35 for everyone else, 65 for coaches. Older players also lose condition faster and get injured more. Rookies fumble more and break fewer tackles than veterans with identical attributes. documented

Part one · contracts

The rookie discount is the whole draft

Veteran salary is almost exactly stars × a position rate — $10.0M per star for a quarterback, down to $3.2M for a kicker. Rookie contracts price at a consistent ~0.6× that rate across all nine positions, which is stable enough to be a single hard-coded discount multiplier.

Extend, with creditsRe-sign at expiry
CostsCoaching creditsNothing
SalaryFreezes at the current numberReprices to his current star rating
Adds+1 year, stackableWhatever the game offers
Rookie yearNot allowedn/a
 Because extension freezes the salary, extending a rising young player during his rookie deal is the best value play in the game — reports of holding a star at $10M a year until he is thirty. Extending an already-maxed five-star is pure waste.

There is no franchise tag, no negotiation, no agent, no holdout. A player takes the number or leaves. What players experience as "he is demanding $50M" is simply the automatic repricing at expiry.

Trade value is startlingly coarse — three tiers: 0.5–1.5 stars is worth a third-round pick, 2.0–3.5 a second, 4.0–5.0 a first. Which creates an obvious and beloved arbitrage: nudging a 3.5-star to 4.0 before the deadline converts a second into a first. Coarse tiers make exploitable seams, and players enjoy finding them. documented

Part one · the rest

Facilities, staff, difficulty and the season

Facilities
Exactly three — Stadium (fan swing), Training (XP rate + morale), Rehab (injury length and frequency, condition). Levels 1–10. They decay over time and need reinvestment, so infrastructure is a treadmill rather than a ratchet. disputed — the wiki says so, the best guide never mentions it.
Staff
Two coordinators, offensive and defensive. 0.5–5.0 stars, two-year contracts, retire at 65. They govern the simulated half of the game, which for the defence is all of it.
Traits
One per coordinator. The community's clear verdict is that the recurring per-game ones win — Physio (+5% condition every game), Likeable (+5% XP every game) — over one-shots like Motivator. Talent Spotter is the exception because it is the only route to a ceiling raise.
Difficulty
Easy, Medium, Hard, Extreme, Dynamic. Extreme simply forces every opponent to play at a 5-star rating regardless of their actual roster. It scales difficulty by falsifying the one scalar the sim already consumes, rather than writing new AI.
Season
16 games plus a bye disputed, then Wild Card, Divisional, Conference Championship, the Retro Bowl. A full game runs under eight minutes at the default two-minute quarters.
What you play
Offence only, plus field goals and kick returns. Defence is a text ticker. You do not pick plays — the game deals you a formation that is simultaneously a run and a pass, and you choose which at the snap.
Dilemmas
Fire 100% of the time after every game and on bye weeks. A guaranteed between-game decision beat.

The developer's own words on why there is no defence

Simon Read: "Every day I get asked for online play, defensive action, full rosters… some of these will change the core of the game too much." And: "Adding major new features risks upsetting the balance of the game and the simplicity that has brought it success."

The cut content is the design. It descends directly from New Star Soccer — play only the decisive moments, manage the rest — and American football is the sport with the cleanest natural split, because it is already platooned. Cutting half of it costs no coherence.

Part one · the absences

What they deliberately do not have

Worth listing, because several are things we have already built and should be confident about.

  • You cannot be fired. Confirmed by the developer. There is no loss condition in the management layer at all. Low fans costs you income, not employment — counter-intuitive enough that most players assume the opposite.
  • No money. No gate receipts, no sponsorship, no season payout. The salary cap is a fixed pool raised only by spending credits.
  • No relegation, no pyramid. Thirty-two teams, flat. The only lateral move is accepting a job at another club, which players use as a self-imposed difficulty reset.
  • No progression gating. No tech tree, no achievement unlocks, no difficulty locked behind seasons. Achievements are pure score-keeping. The only thing between a new player and a maxed franchise is the credit drip.
  • No penalties, no punting input, no defensive call.

Part three · the take

What to steal, ranked

Ordered by how much they would change the game per hour of work, most first. The numbers are a sequence here because that is what the ranking is.

Make notoriety pay every event, not once a year

This is the single biggest finding and it answers your original question directly. Their fan meter multiplies per-game income by three. Ours currently pays a flat 2 credits for a win no matter how famous the club is — notoriety only reaches the wallet through the yearly tournament and a small season-end gate. That makes our most interesting meter almost invisible in play.

Take their banding. Something like 1 credit below 25 notoriety, 2 to 50, 3 to 75, 4 above — which conveniently lands on the division gates you already set. Now every fight pays a number the player watches climb, and the whole fans-and-notoriety system starts doing work every week instead of every summer.

Cost: a few lines in _after_event and a balance pass. Risk: total credit income roughly doubles at the top, so the sinks need re-pricing against it.

Fighters have to age and retire

We have no age, no decline and no retirement anywhere in the codebase. That is the biggest structural hole in the game as a career, because it means a squad you build once is a squad you keep forever, and the reserve never has a reason to exist. Retro Bowl's is confirmed and brutally simple: decline from 31, forced retirement at 35, quarterbacks and kickers at 40.

Buhurt's own shape would be different and better — this is a sport people fight into their forties, and the stat that goes first is gas, not strength. Decline gas and speed-adjacent stats from about 33, retire around 42, and the heavy Rails last longer than the Flankers.

Cost: an age field on FighterCard, a decline pass in roll_over, retirement handling, and the squad screen showing it. Unlocks: makes the reserve, the training ground and the draft all matter at once.

Potential, as a second visible number

Their entire long game is "draft the 2.5-star with 4.5-star potential". We have no equivalent — a fighter is simply as good as he is. Adding a potential rating to FighterCard turns every signing and every created fighter into a bet rather than a purchase, and it gives the Training ground something to push against.

Their Talent Spotter lesson is worth taking too: make exactly one thing raise the ceiling, and make it scarce. A captain speciality, or a facility level, but only one road.

Cost: a field, a training rework, two screens. Pairs with: ageing — potential is meaningless without a clock.

A replacement-level fighter, and a smaller squad

Their noname is the best idea in their roster design. Our squeeze is soft: 13 men for 5 positions, so the line is always fillable and depth is comfortable. Theirs is 10 men for 23 slots with a guaranteed disaster in every gap.

We should not copy the number — a buhurt club genuinely carries a squad — but we should copy the pressure. An injured or suspended fighter with nobody behind him should put a visibly terrible stand-in on the line, named as such, rather than quietly shuffling the depth chart. It makes the eight feel thin in exactly the way a real club feels thin.

Cost: small. starting_five already falls back by role — it just needs to fall back to something bad and say so.

A decision between every fight

Their dilemma fires 100% of the time after every game and on every bye. It costs the player ten seconds, moves morale or fans by a little, and occasionally pays a credit. We have nothing at all between fights — you finish a bout and go straight back to the table.

For buhurt the material is right there: a fighter's kit failed inspection, a marshal's call you can contest, another club wants a friendly, a captain disagrees with the line. One binary choice, one small consequence, every event.

Cost: a small data table and one panel. Value: it is the cheapest pacing device on this whole list.

Contracts, with the re-sign versus extend fork

Our wages are derived from overall and never expire, so the salary cap is a static constraint rather than a rolling decision. Theirs makes it a yearly fight: extend early with currency and freeze the price, or let it run and pay the market rate for the fighter he has become.

That one fork generates most of their long-game texture, and the rookie discount at ~0.6× is what makes bringing men through worth doing.

Cost: the largest item on this list — contract length, expiry, a re-sign phase in the summer. Do it after ageing, or there is nothing for a contract to be about.

Facility decay, or a deliberate decision not to

Theirs decay, so infrastructure is a treadmill and equilibrium is never reached. Ours are permanent, so the Clubhouse is a checklist you finish and never look at again. Either is defensible, but right now ours is an accident rather than a choice.

Buhurt's version writes itself: a training ground and an infirmary need maintaining, and a ground that stands empty for a season is a ground that needs work. It also gives credits somewhere to go late, when the arena is built and there is nothing left to buy.

Cost: trivial. Watch: decay plus a per-week throttle is how they stop windfalls collapsing pacing — worth taking both or neither.

Coarse tiers, so the seams are findable

Their trade value has three brackets, which means nudging a fighter over a boundary converts a cheap asset into an expensive one. Every community guide has found this and enjoys it. Our numbers are mostly continuous and smooth, which is fairer and much less fun to optimise.

Worth deliberately introducing a couple of coarse boundaries somewhere — signing value, or what the federation pays for a podium — purely so there is something to game.

Cost: none, it is a re-shape of numbers we already have.

Part four · hold the line

What we already do better

Not everything they do is worth copying, and some of our differences are the reason to build this game at all.

We have real stakes

They cannot be fired and there is no pyramid. Relegation is a genuine fail state and promotion is a genuine reward, and it is what makes our arena gating and our notoriety curve mean something. Keep it.

Our crowd model is richer than theirs

They have one number with three bands. We have a following that is built over years and a turnout that decides how much of it shows up, with a 25% ceiling that produces a genuine sold-out-and-turning-people-away state. That is better — it just needs to pay out more often.

Our cap scales with the league

Theirs is flat and purchasable, which makes money a pure credit sink. Ours moves with the division, so promotion changes what a club can afford. That is the more interesting system.

The tactics layer is ours

They have audibles. We have formations, strategies, a Chalkboard and a measured ladder that says the roster beats the thumb and the thumb beats the tactics. Nothing in Retro Bowl is trying to do this, and it is the clearest reason for our game to exist.

We measure

Every number in their game is community reverse-engineering, and some of it is wrong. Every number in ours has a check behind it. That is worth more the longer this project runs.

We should not copy their monetisation shape

Their early-game credit bottleneck is priced against a $0.99 button — the fan meter is the monetisation curve with a crowd drawn on it, and the most-cited criticism of the game is that the first five seasons are friction. A premium game has no reason to inherit that ramp. Take the banding, not the scarcity.

Caveats

How much of this to trust

Retro Bowl has shipped continuously since 2020 and has three spin-offs. Most precise figures come from 2021–22 community guides, so treat them as period-accurate rather than current. The developer has never commented on the fan meter, the credit economy or their tuning — everything about why the fan system works as it does is analysis, mine or the community's, not a statement of intent.

Three specific holes worth knowing: the actual fan percentage per win and per loss is genuinely unknown at every stadium level; injury durations have no published table and sources contradict each other; and the playoff field size is undocumented. Reddit and parts of the wiki were unreachable during this research, so some gaps are access gaps rather than absences.
